Signal not Noise · Brisbane · a rethink of the 25 minutes

Fool the room. Then teach it to never be fooled again.

The strongest version of this talk is not a tour of an eval app. It is a story where the room is the hero: they get fooled by a demo in minute three, they write the rules in minute eight, their rules become the judges in minute fourteen, and they overrule a judge in minute nineteen. The app is the thing being tamed. The screen shows one object at a time, and judgement visibly moves from applause to evidence.

This page is the playback for review: the challenges to the current framing, the proposed arc, a frame-by-frame storyboard with the presenter line for each beat, the crowd moments and their fallbacks, and the build plan. Frames are drawn in the proposed stage language, with the PM telling as the running example.

Five challenges to the current thinking

The app is the hero. It should be the obstacle.

Today the audience watches you operate software: pick a phrase, press a key, wait, read. That is a tutorial, and tutorials feel like "a supercharged PowerPoint" however good the UI is. Busy screens are the symptom; the cause is that the app carries the story.

So: make the room the protagonist. Every act is something the room does to the translator, and the screen shows their judgement as a first-class object.

The game show is the villain, not the format.

A full game-show frame is fun for three minutes and cheesy for twenty-five. But an applause-o-meter is exactly the thing evals replace: judgement by vibes.

So: open as a game show, then break it. The applause meter stays on screen as "Vibes" and withers as "Evidence" grows. Leaving the game show behind is the story.

The fooled moment is buried in act 1. It is the hook.

The retellable moment of this talk is "the whole room cheered, and nobody noticed the notice period had halved". Right now that happens quietly in a five-phrase list.

So: get the room to vote "ship it" on a real notice, then zoom in on the number that changed. "You all just approved that." Thesis delivered in minute three, with the room complicit.

Eighteen ideas is a course. Four is a talk.

Prioritise, define, collect, rubric, golden, invariants, three judge types, context, pictures, voice, gates. The previous review counted eighteen named ideas. A room keeps about four.

So: four movements, each with one idea the room can say back: vibes miss what matters · say what good looks like (with bad examples) · turn your rules into tireless judges · check the checker and go round again. Pictures and voice go.

One honest risk: the PM persona is told to change numbers.

The PM prompt asks the model to "second-guess the numbers". When "4 weeks" becomes "2 weeks", a sceptic says you asked for it. The Aussie telling's "tinnies" is emergent drift, which is the real lesson.

So: keep the PM voice, but test a Version 1 that describes only the voice ("confident, know-it-all, talks to the dev team") and see if the numbers still drift. If they do, PM wins. If not, the fooled moment needs the Aussie telling. Decision below.

Live rule-writing is lower risk than it feels.

Ask "what must never change when we translate a notice?" and every room says the same five things: the numbers, the dates, the rules, don't add anything, don't be rude. You already have judges for all five.

So: do it live, with the five cards pre-built and the room's wording typed onto them. Anything new goes on a dashed "later" card, which is itself a lesson about prioritising.

The arc

Five movements. The two meters are the spine: Vibes starts full and Evidence starts empty, and they cross in movement three. The loop is the one mental model, and it is drawn with a gap until the room fills it.

Orange dots are the six moments the room acts. Each one changes what is on screen, and the room can see its own judgement land.

The stage grammar

One layout for the whole talk, so the room never re-learns the screen. Everything that is not the current object is gone: no instruction panel, no phrase list, no footer dots, no stepper of six words. Those move to the presenter view.

  • The card sits in the middle: the English in grey, what came out in white, at 50-plus point. It is the only thing the room reads.
  • Judgement arrives from the edges. The room's vote appears as a bar under the card. Later, the judges appear as a row of stamps under the card. The instruction note slides in from the left only when the instructions are the point.
  • The HUD is two things: the loop glyph top-left (four dots, the current station lit, the "define" dot dashed until the room fills it) and the two meters top-right, big enough to read from the back.
  • Progressive disclosure. The "full lab" exists only on the last working screen, and the room watched every piece arrive. That is the mental model: the lab was built in front of them, by them.
  • Transitions carry meaning. Rule cards morph into judge stamps. Answers fly into the good/borderline/bad columns. The loop glyph completes a lap when the feedback is applied. One orchestrated move per beat, nothing ambient.

Three decisions for you

1. PM-speak or Aussie for the fooled moment?

PM lands the self-deprecating laugh with this room and makes every failure a familiar one. The risk is that the Version 1 prompt tells the model to second-guess numbers, so the reveal is "you asked for it". Before committing, run a Version 1 that describes only the voice and check whether "4 weeks" still drifts. If it does, PM. If it doesn't, the reveal uses the Aussie "a couple of tinnies" and the rest of the talk stays PM, or the whole talk goes Aussie. The app already supports both tellings, so this is a half-hour test, not a rebuild.

2. The applause-o-meter: mic or hand-driven?

The mic version is a genuine laugh and needs no network, but conference AV sometimes blocks laptop mics. Build both: mic by default, V swings it by hand, and rehearse the hand version so it looks deliberate.

3. Keep 3.4 (context) or bank the ninety seconds?

It is a good idea and a weak beat: a fourth failure type after the room has already got the point. The storyboard keeps it as the first cut. My lean is to cut it and spend the time on 2.3, letting the room argue about answer B for longer, which is the moment they will retell.

What this means for the build

This is a presentation-layer rebuild on the existing machinery. The runner, store, replay mode, judges, variants, presenter window and server stay. What changes is what the projector shows: a stage with one object per beat, a persistent HUD, and crowd controls.

Keep as is
Translator and judge calls, replay recording, lab state, the three tellings and their prompts, golden examples and the act 6 review flow, the presenter window, the dev-server key handling, act budgets and the timer.
Phase 1 · Stage shell
Strip the projector chrome (stepper, footer dots, key badges go to the presenter view). New HUD: loop glyph with four stations and the dashed Define, meters at readable size. Stage typography scale. One transition system (cards fly, stamps land, glyph laps) with reduced-motion fallback.
  • Touches: App.tsx, TopBar.tsx, styles.css, a new Stage.tsx
Phase 2 · New scene kinds
Each beat above is a scene kind with its own component, fed by the existing variant data.
  • Build: typed instruction note plus the card (1.2)
  • Card: one phrase, highlight-on-click, deal five (1.4, 1.5)
  • Loop: the gap, then the lap (2.1, 4.3)
  • Rules: live-typed cards with seals and the "not now" card (2.2)
  • Vote: answers with presenter-set bars and reveal (2.3, 2.4, 4.1)
  • Wall: the golden columns (2.5), replacing Golden.tsx
  • Judged card: card plus stamp row and verdict (3.2, 3.4), replacing the right half of Lab.tsx
  • Bars: version tally, before/after, scoreboard, gates (3.3, 4.3, 4.4, 5.1)
  • Bins: the two feedback bins (4.2), a slimmer Review.tsx plus Improve.tsx
Removed from the running order and kept in code: pictures, voice, the kitchen-sink version.
Phase 3 · Crowd controls
Applause dial (Web Audio on the mic, hand-driven fallback), vote bars on keys 1–5, thumbs on arrows, live rule typing with pre-built cards, the prepared-note shortcut. All recorded for replay.
Phase 4 · Presenter view and docs
Beats per scene with the keys in bold, the crowd controls as buttons, the act clock against the new budgets. Rewrite the run of show and the talk track to this storyboard; retire the old scene list.
Phase 5 · Rehearse
Walk it with recording on, screenshot every beat at 1080p and check from the back of a room, then present from replay. The PM-vs-Aussie test in decision 1 happens before Phase 2 starts.

Rough size: Phases 1 to 3 are four or five focused sessions, Phase 4 one, and the content (prompts, examples, judges) is already written and tested. The biggest unknown is the persona test; the second is how the mic behaves on the venue's AV.
